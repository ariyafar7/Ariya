# Michael the Microbe: evidence pack

Every question in the game should be backed by research. This pack does three things:

1. Analyses the two core readings (Qin 2014, Vicario 2012): what still holds and what's out of date.
2. Lists newer research and guidelines that update or strengthen them.
3. Gives each game stop the claim it teaches, the evidence behind it, a plain-language **"Read the research"** write-up for players, and links.

**How this was checked:** I verified the newer sources through their abstracts, publisher pages and secondary summaries, not the full texts. Before final submission, someone from MEDN should open each linked paper and confirm the numbers quoted here. Items marked **⚠ verify** came from general knowledge or a secondary source and still need a proper citation.

### Evidence strength badges (for players)

| Badge | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ●●● Strong | Clinical guideline, or a review that pools many studies | BSG 2021 guideline, meta-analyses |
| ●●○ Good | Study in people | Vanuytsel 2014, Koloski 2012 |
| ●○○ Early | Animal study, or a narrative (non-systematic) review | Vicario 2012 (rats), Qin 2014 |

Showing a badge next to each "Read the research" link tells players how much weight to give a finding. It's also good science communication, and it helps your science marks.

## 1. The two core readings

### Qin et al. 2014: Impact of psychological stress on irritable bowel syndrome
*World J Gastroenterol* 20(39):14126–31 · [doi:10.3748/wjg.v20.i39.14126](https://doi.org/10.3748/wjg.v20.i39.14126) · ●○○ narrative review

**Still useful:**
- A clear overview of the main pathways: the CRF/HPA stress chain, mast cells, serotonin cells, gut-lining permeability, microbiota, and the two-way ("top-down" and "bottom-up") brain–gut loop
- A list of management approaches: education, CBT, hypnotherapy, diet, exercise, medicines

**Out of date or weak:**
- **The 11.2% prevalence figure is outdated.** It comes from a 2012 meta-analysis that used older, looser diagnostic criteria. Under the current Rome IV criteria, about **4%** of people worldwide have IBS (see section 2).
- It's a narrative review, not a systematic one, and much of its mechanism evidence comes from animal studies.
- Its treatment section predates the current guidelines (BSG 2021, see section 2).

**How to use it:** keep it as the "big picture" source, but back each specific claim with a newer or stronger source below.

### Vicario et al. 2012: Chronic psychosocial stress … in the rat intestine and IBS-like gut dysfunction
*Psychoneuroendocrinology* 37:65–77 · [doi:10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) · ●○○ animal study

**Key findings:** rats crowded 8 to a cage for 15 days, compared with 2 per cage, had:
- stress hormone about 3.5× higher (165 vs 47 ng/mL)
- more frequent, mushier poo during the stress period
- a leakier, more reactive gut lining that lasted up to 7 days after the stress ended
- an oversensitive gut that lasted up to **30 days** after the stress ended
- about 4× more CRF receptor type-1 in the colon. The most stressed rats had the most receptors.
- damaged energy factories (mitochondria) in gut cells, which recovered

**Strengths:**
- Crowding is a realistic social stressor, closer to everyday human stress than lab stressors like electric shocks.
- It tracks *time*, showing some changes recover quickly while others linger.

**Limitations (worth naming in your reflective report):**
- Male rats only, but IBS is more common in women.
- A stress-sensitive rat strain (Wistar–Kyoto).
- Small groups of about 6 to 16 rats each.
- Rat findings may not carry over to people.

**How to use it:** good for "here's what stress can do over time". Always label it as a rat study in the game.

### Changes to make in the game because of this
1. **Replace "1 in 9 people (11.2%)"** with "about 1 in 25 people worldwide (around 4%) under today's diagnostic criteria". Add that it's more common in women, and that some studies find it more often in medical students. Student figures vary a lot between studies, so don't generalise to all students.
2. **Add the public-speech study (Vanuytsel 2014).** It's human evidence, and a speech or presentation is exactly the kind of stress your audience faces.
3. **Soften the microbiota claims.** Say "stress can shift the balance of gut bacteria, and scientists are still working out how much this matters for IBS", not that stress definitely causes dysbiosis in people.
4. **Probiotics:** "may help some people; try one for up to 12 weeks, ideally with advice". Don't present them as a fix. Guidelines differ.
5. **Diet:** soluble fibre and regular meals first; low-FODMAP only with a dietitian.

## 3. Evidence for each game stop

Each stop follows Sam's story. For each one: the takeaway, the evidence behind it, and a **"Read the research"** write-up written for players. The write-ups use plain language, and each is under 100 words, so it fits in the game.

### Stop 1 · Mouth: "Alarm mode"
**Takeaway:** a dry mouth and a clenched jaw are signs of stress in your body. Notice them early.

**Evidence:** stress and awake jaw clenching (Front Neurol 2020 review ●●○). Dry mouth under stress comes from the body's "fight or flight" nerves turning down saliva (⚠ needs a source).

> **Read the research**
> When you're stressed, your body switches into "alarm mode". Digestion goes on hold, which is why your mouth can feel dry. Studies also link stress and anxiety to clenching your jaw during the day, more than grinding at night. These are some of the first signs that stress is showing up in your body, not just your head.
> 📄 [Stress and bruxism: systematic review, Frontiers in Neurology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2020.590779/full) ●●○

**Also at this stop: what IBS is**

> **Read the research**
> IBS (irritable bowel syndrome) causes repeated tummy pain together with diarrhoea, constipation or both. Under today's diagnostic rules, about 4 in every 100 people worldwide have it, and it's more common in women. Some studies find it more often in medical students. Tests usually show a normal-looking gut, but the symptoms are real.
> 📄 [Oka et al. 2020, global prevalence of IBS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32702295/) ●●● · 📄 [IBS in medical students, meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12745469/) ●●●

### Stop 2 · Oesophagus: "Your brain and gut talk both ways"
**Takeaway:** gut feelings are real signals. Stress shows up in your belly, and belly trouble can make you more anxious.

**Evidence:** Koloski 2012 ●●○ (human, 12 years); Qin 2014 ●○○; Cryan 2019 ●●●.

> **Read the research**
> Researchers followed 1,002 Australian adults for 12 years. People who were anxious at the start were more likely to develop gut problems later. People who started with gut problems were more likely to become anxious or low later on. So the brain affects the gut, and the gut affects the brain. It's a two-way street.
> 📄 [Koloski et al. 2012, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/61/9/1284) ●●○

### Stop 3 · Stomach: "Stress hormones upset your stomach"
**Takeaway:** losing your appetite under stress is common. Small, simple, regular meals beat skipping meals.

**Evidence:** HPA chain (Qin 2014 ●○○); CRF changes stomach and bowel movement (Taché & Bonaz 2007 ●●○); stress hormone about 3.5× higher in stressed rats (Vicario 2012 ●○○). Regular meals as first-line IBS diet advice (BSG 2021 ●●● ⚠ confirm wording).

> **Read the research**
> When your brain senses stress, it sends a chain of signals: CRF, then ACTH, then cortisol, the main stress hormone. These signals slow and unsettle the stomach, which is why you might feel sick or lose your appetite before an exam. In one study, rats living in crowded cages for two weeks had about three and a half times more stress hormone than calm rats.
> 📄 [Taché & Bonaz 2007, J Clin Invest](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI30085) ●●○ · 📄 [Vicario et al. 2012 (rats)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) ●○○

### Stop 4 · Small intestine: "Stress makes the gut lining let more through"
**Takeaway:** a short burst of stress passes. It's long stretches with no break that wear the gut down, so build rest into busy periods.

**Evidence:** Vanuytsel 2014 ●●○ (public speech, humans); Vicario 2012 ●○○ (lasted 7 days in rats); Barbara 2004 ●●○ (mast cells near nerves). Caution from La Torre 2023.

> **Read the research**
> Your gut lining is a wall one cell thick. When healthy volunteers had to give a public speech, their small intestine became leakier, but only in people whose stress hormone rose. A drug that calms "alarm cells" in the gut wall (mast cells) stopped this happening. In rats, two weeks of crowding kept the lining leaky for up to a week after the stress ended. Scientists are still checking how strong this effect is in everyday life.
> 📄 [Vanuytsel et al. 2014, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/63/8/1293) ●●○ · 📄 [Vicario et al. 2012 (rats)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) ●○○

### Stop 5 · Ileum: "Gut messengers set the pace"
**Takeaway:** toilet changes in stressful times are common. Tracking when they happen helps you spot your triggers.

**Evidence:** about 90–95% of serotonin made in the gut (Gershon & Tack 2007 ⚠ verify); serotonin controls movement, fluid and pain signals (Qin 2014 ●○○). Symptom tracking (⚠ needs a source, e.g. a guideline or dietitian resource).

> **Read the research**
> Most of your body's serotonin, about 9 out of every 10 molecules, is made by cells in your gut lining, not your brain. In the gut, serotonin controls how fast food moves, how much fluid is released and how strongly pain signals travel. Stress can change these signals, so things can speed up (diarrhoea) or slow down (constipation). That's why some IBS medicines act on serotonin.
> 📄 [Gershon & Tack 2007, Gastroenterology] ⚠ add link after verifying · 📄 [Qin et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3748/wjg.v20.i39.14126) ●○○

### Stop 6 · Colon: "Your gut bacteria and oversensitive nerves"
**Takeaway:** the pain is real, not imagined. A GP or dietitian can help you find what works.

**Evidence:**
- Bacteria differ in IBS (Pittayanon 2019 ●●●)
- How microbes and the brain communicate (Cryan 2019 ●●●)
- Exam stress and gut bacteria, mixed results (2025 RCT ●●○)
- Nerves turned up: Dickhaus 2003 ●●○, Barbara 2004 ●●○, and 30 days in rats (Vicario 2012 ●○○)

> **Read the research**
> Your colon is home to trillions of bacteria. People with IBS tend to have a different mix of gut bacteria, and studies of students suggest exam stress can shift some species. Scientists are still working out how much this matters. Stress can also turn up the "volume" on gut nerves, so normal gas or stretching feels painful. In people with IBS, immune "alarm cells" sit unusually close to gut nerves, and this is linked to more pain.
> 📄 [Pittayanon et al. 2019](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1636046) ●●● · 📄 [Barbara et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2003.11.055) ●●○ · 📄 [Students, exam stress and gut bacteria, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41522-025-00776-w) ●●○

### Stop 7 · Exit: "Break the loop"
**Takeaway:** calm the mind and look after the gut. Know the warning signs that need a doctor.

**Evidence:** Black 2020 ●●● (CBT, hypnotherapy); BSG 2021 ●●● (exercise, fibre, low-FODMAP with a dietitian, probiotics trial); Johannesson 2011 ●●○ (exercise). Red-flag symptoms (BSG 2021 ⚠ confirm the list).

> **Read the research**
> Because stress and the gut are linked, the best care treats both. Across 41 trials, talking therapies like CBT (including online and self-guided versions) and gut-directed hypnotherapy reduced IBS symptoms. In one trial, people who exercised 20 to 30 minutes, 3 to 5 times a week, had clearly fewer symptoms after 3 months. UK guidelines recommend regular exercise for everyone with IBS, soluble fibre, and help from a dietitian before trying stricter diets.
> 📄 [Black et al. 2020, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/8/1441) ●●● · 📄 [BSG IBS guideline 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33903147/) ●●● · 📄 [Johannesson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/ajg.2010.480) ●●○
