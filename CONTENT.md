## Title card (not graded): what is IBS?

> **Did you know?** About 1 in 25 people worldwide have IBS (irritable bowel syndrome). It causes repeated tummy pain together with diarrhoea, constipation or both. Tests usually show a normal-looking gut, but the symptoms are real. It's more common in women, and some studies find it more often in students.

**Read the research**
> Doctors now diagnose IBS using a set of criteria called "Rome IV". A review that pooled studies covering 82,476 people in 34 countries found about 3.8% of people meet those criteria. Older, looser criteria gave about 9%, which is why you may see higher numbers quoted elsewhere. Studies of medical students often find higher rates, but results vary a lot between studies.
> 📄 [Oka et al. 2020, Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32702295/) ●●● · 📄 [IBS in medical students, meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12745469/) ●●●

## Stop 1 · Mouth (0.0 m): alarm mode
**Interaction:** scenario multiple choice
**Michael says:** "Teeth ahead! This is where digestion starts, and where stress often shows up first."

**Scenario:** *It's the night before Sam's first exam. Their mouth feels dry and their jaw aches from clenching.*
**What's going on?**
- A) Sam's coming down with a cold
- **B) Sam's body has switched into "alarm mode" ✔**
- C) Sam needs to brush their teeth more
- D) It's unrelated to stress

**Feedback (right):** Stress flips your body into alarm mode. Digestion goes on hold, so saliva drops, and many people clench their jaw without noticing.
**Feedback (wrong):** These are classic stress signs. Your body is in alarm mode, getting ready to act instead of digest.
**Tip:** Notice the signs early. Unclench your jaw, sip some water, and breathe out slowly.

**Read the research**
> When you're stressed, your nervous system switches into "fight or flight". It turns down jobs like digestion, which is why your mouth can go dry. Reviews of the research link stress and anxiety to clenching your jaw during the day, more than grinding at night. Noticing these early signs is the first step to managing stress.
> 📄 [Stress and bruxism: systematic review, Frontiers in Neurology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2020.590779/full) ●●○ · ⚠ add a source for stress and dry mouth

## Stop 2 · Oesophagus (0.25 m): a two-way street
**Interaction:** true/false quick-fire (3 statements)
**Michael says:** "This tube is lined with nerves, and nerves carry messages between your gut and brain."

*Sam feels a lump in their throat walking into the exam hall.*

| Statement | Answer | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| "Stress only affects your mind, not your body." | **False** | Stress changes how your gut moves, feels and works. |
| "Gut problems can make you feel more anxious." | **True** | The gut sends signals to the brain too. It's a two-way street. |
| "Your gut has its own network of nerves." | **True** | It's sometimes called your "second brain". |

**Tip:** "Gut feelings" are real signals. Looking after one end helps the other.

**Read the research**
> Researchers followed 1,002 Australian adults for 12 years. People who were anxious at the start were more likely to develop gut problems later. People who started with gut problems were more likely to become anxious later. Your gut talks to your brain through nerves (like the vagus nerve), hormones, immune signals and chemicals made by gut bacteria.
> 📄 [Koloski et al. 2012, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/61/9/1284) ●●○ · 📄 [Cryan et al. 2019, The microbiota-gut-brain axis](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00018.2018) ●●●

## Stop 3 · Stomach (0.45 m): the stress chain
**Interaction:** put in order (tap the steps in sequence)
**Michael says:** "Splash! Stomach acid! And the stress signal from Sam's brain just arrived."

*Sam can't face breakfast on exam morning. Put the stress chain in order:*
1. Sam's brain spots the stress (hypothalamus)
2. A gland under the brain passes the message on (pituitary)
3. Glands on top of the kidneys release the stress hormone cortisol (adrenal glands)
4. Sam's stomach slows down and their appetite drops

**Feedback:** That's the body's stress alarm system. It's useful for a short burst, but if it stays switched on for weeks, the gut feels it.
**Tip:** Losing your appetite under stress is common. Something small and plain beats skipping meals altogether.

**Read the research**
> Stress messages travel from the brain to the adrenal glands, which release cortisol. The brain's first messenger in this chain (called CRF) also acts directly on the gut, changing how fast the stomach and bowel move. In one study, rats kept in crowded cages for two weeks had about three and a half times more stress hormone than calm rats. This was in rats, but it shows the chain at work.
> 📄 [Taché & Bonaz 2007, J Clin Invest (free)](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI30085) ●●○ · 📄 [Vicario et al. 2012 (rats)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) ●○○

## Stop 4 · Small intestine (0.75–2.0 m): the gut lining
**Interaction:** scenario multiple choice
**Michael says:** "Most food is absorbed here, through a wall just one cell thick."

**Scenario:** *Sam has to give a class presentation, and their heart is racing.*
**What's most likely happening in Sam's small intestine?**
- A) Nothing. The intestine can't sense stress.
- **B) The gut lining briefly lets more through, especially if Sam's stress hormone spikes ✔**
- C) The intestine stops working completely
- D) The gut lining gets thicker and stronger

**Feedback (right):** In a study of healthy volunteers, a public speech made the gut lining leakier, but only in people whose stress hormone rose. A short burst like this usually settles.
**Feedback (wrong):** The gut does react. Studies show stress can make the gut lining let more through for a while.
**Tip:** Short stress passes. It's weeks of stress with no break that wear the gut down, so build rest into busy periods.

**Read the research**
> Healthy volunteers who had to give a public speech developed a leakier small intestine, but only those whose stress hormone went up. A drug that calms "alarm cells" in the gut wall (mast cells) stopped this happening. In rats, two weeks of crowding kept the gut lining leaky for up to a week after the stress ended. Scientists are still checking how big this effect is in everyday life.
> 📄 [Vanuytsel et al. 2014, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/63/8/1293) ●●○ · 📄 [Vicario et al. 2012 (rats)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) ●○○

## Stop 5 · Ileum (4.5 m): gut messengers
**Interaction:** multiple choice (fill the gap)
**Michael says:** "This is the longest stretch, packed with cells that make chemical messengers."

*Sam has been rushing to the toilet all week.*
**Most of the body's serotonin, a key messenger chemical, is made in the…**
- A) brain
- **B) gut ✔**
- C) blood
- D) skin

**Feedback:** About 9 out of every 10 serotonin molecules are made in the gut lining. In the gut, serotonin controls how fast things move and how strongly pain signals travel. Stress can change these signals, so things can speed up (diarrhoea) or slow down (constipation).
**Tip:** Toilet changes during stressful weeks are common. Noting when they happen helps you spot your triggers.

**Read the research**
> Special cells in the gut lining make most of the body's serotonin. In the gut it controls movement, fluid release and pain signals, rather than mood. That's why some IBS medicines target serotonin, and why low-dose antidepressants are sometimes prescribed for IBS. They act on gut–brain signals, not only on mood.
> 📄 Gershon & Tack 2007, Gastroenterology ⚠ verify and add the link · 📄 [Qin et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3748/wjg.v20.i39.14126) ●○○

## Stop 6 · Colon (7.2 m): Michael's home, and the probiotics myth
**Interaction:** scenario multiple choice (myth-buster)
**Michael says:** "Welcome to the big city. Trillions of microbes live here, including me!"

**Scenario:** *Sam's flatmate says, "Just take a probiotic. It fixes stress."*
**What does the research actually say?**
- A) Probiotics cure stress and anxiety
- **B) Some may slightly ease stress in healthy adults, but in young people they haven't been shown to help anxiety. They're no substitute for sleep, movement and support. ✔**
- C) Probiotics are dangerous for everyone
- D) Any yoghurt works the same as a probiotic supplement

**Feedback (right):** Probiotics are not a fix. Effects are small and depend on the exact strain. For IBS symptoms, guidelines say a probiotic can be tried for up to 12 weeks. If it doesn't help, stop.
**Feedback (wrong):** The evidence is more modest. Some probiotics may slightly ease felt stress, but trials in young people found no clear effect on anxiety.
**Tip:** Feed your gut community with regular meals and fibre that suits you. Treat probiotics as an optional extra, not a cure.

**Read the research**
> Stress can shift the mix of bacteria in your gut. Studies of students during exams find some changes, though results vary. People with IBS tend to have a different mix of gut bacteria, but scientists haven't yet shown that this causes symptoms. A review of 7 trials found probiotics may slightly lower felt stress in healthy adults, with no effect on cortisol. A review of trials in 10- to 24-year-olds found no clear effect on anxiety.
> 📄 [Zhang et al. 2020, Brain and Behavior](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32662591/) ●●● · 📄 [Cohen Kadosh et al. 2021, Translational Psychiatry](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8206413/) ●●● · 📄 [Pittayanon et al. 2019](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1636046) ●●● · 📄 [Exam stress and gut bacteria, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41522-025-00776-w) ●●○

**Want more? (optional deeper card)**
> **Fibre, fermented foods and your gut.** Fibre that your gut bacteria can ferment helps them make substances that support the gut lining and may calm inflammation. For people with IBS, the type of fibre matters. Soluble fibre (such as oats or psyllium) tends to help, while wheat bran can make symptoms worse. Some high-fibre foods, like beans and onions, can trigger IBS symptoms, so a dietitian can help you find what suits you. Fermented foods (yoghurt, kefir, kimchi) are promising but not proven to ease stress.
> 📄 [BSG IBS guideline 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33903147/) ●●● · 📄 [La Torre, Verbeke & Dalile 2021, fibre and the gut–brain axis (free)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11406392/) ●○○ · 📄 [Marx et al. 2017, nutritional psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0029665117002026) ●○○

## Stop 7 · Sigmoid colon (8.4 m): the pain is real
**Interaction:** tap the scene (fallback: true/false)
**Michael says:** "Look! Some alarm cells are parked right next to a nerve."

**Tap version:** *Tap the alarm cell that's turning up Sam's pain signals.* (The player taps the mast cell sitting beside the nerve fibre.)
**True/false version:** "If stress makes IBS worse, then IBS is all in your head." **False**

**Feedback:** Stress can turn up the "volume" on gut nerves, so normal gas or stretching hurts. In people with IBS, immune alarm cells sit unusually close to gut nerves, and this is linked to more pain. The pain is real.
**Tip:** If gut pain keeps coming back, see a GP. It's real, and there's help.

**Read the research**
> In people with IBS, activated alarm cells (mast cells) were found closer to gut nerves than usual, and this was linked to how much pain people reported. Under stress, people with IBS also felt gut stretching more strongly than people without IBS. In rats, gut oversensitivity lasted up to 30 days after the stress ended.
> 📄 [Barbara et al. 2004, Gastroenterology](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2003.11.055) ●●○ · 📄 [Dickhaus et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1572-0241.2003.07156.x) ●●○ · 📄 [Vicario et al. 2012 (rats)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.005) ●○○

## Stop 8 · Exit (8.9 m): choose Sam's path
**Interaction:** choose Sam's path (3 decisions). Two meters change with each choice: 🧠 stress and 🦠 gut.
**Michael says:** "Final stretch! You decide what Sam does next."

| Moment | Option A | Option B (better) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Night before the last exam | Pull an all-nighter with energy drinks | Revise until a set time, then sleep at the usual hour | Poor sleep raises stress, and sleep and gut bacteria are linked (correlational) |
| Exam-day lunch | Skip it, "no time" | Have something small and plain | Regular meals are first-line IBS advice |
| The week after exams | Stay in and scroll | Brisk walk with a friend, 20–30 minutes | Exercise reduced IBS symptoms in a trial, and support helps stress |

**Feedback:** Each better choice lowers both meters. The ending shows Sam calmer, with a happier gut community.

**Read the research**
> Across 41 trials, talking therapies like CBT (including online versions) and gut-directed hypnotherapy reduced IBS symptoms. In one trial, people with IBS who exercised 20 to 30 minutes, 3 to 5 times a week, had clearly fewer symptoms after 3 months. UK guidelines recommend regular exercise for everyone with IBS. A small study linked better sleep with a more varied gut community. It's a link, not proof of cause, but sleep clearly matters for stress.
> 📄 [Black et al. 2020, Gut](https://gut.bmj.com/content/69/8/1441) ●●● · 📄 [Johannesson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/ajg.2010.480) ●●○ · 📄 [BSG IBS guideline 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33903147/) ●●● · 📄 [Smith et al. 2019, PLoS One (free)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222394) ●●○
